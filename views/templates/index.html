How the BB84 Quantum Key Distribution Protocol Works

Proposed in 1984 by Charles H. Bennett and Gilles Brassard, BB84 is the foundational protocol for Quantum Key Distribution (QKD). It allows two remote parties—traditionally Alice and Bob—to establish a shared, unconditionally secret random key over an insecure quantum channel.

Unlike classical cryptographic algorithms whose security depends on unproven computational complexity assumptions (such as integer factorization or discrete logarithms), BB84 derives information-theoretic security directly from the laws of quantum mechanics:

Quantum Superposition

Qubits prepared in non-orthogonal quantum states cannot be measured without perturbing their state, causing irreversible wave-function collapse.

No-Cloning Theorem

It is mathematically impossible for an eavesdropper (Eve) to create an exact copy of an unknown, arbitrary quantum state without leaving detectable traces.

Protocol Execution Stages
  1. State Preparation: Alice generates random classical bits $b_i \in \{0, 1\}$ and randomly chooses either the rectilinear basis $\boxplus$ ($\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$) or diagonal basis $\boxtimes$ ($\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$) to encode each bit into a polarized photon.
  2. Channel Transmission: Photons travel through the quantum channel, subject to potential environmental depolarizing noise or eavesdropper interception.
  3. Projective Measurement: Bob independently chooses random measurement bases for each incoming qubit and records the projected classical outcomes.
  4. Sifting Phase: Over an authenticated classical channel, Alice and Bob openly compare their chosen bases (without revealing the bits). Bits measured with discordant bases are discarded, yielding the sifted key.
  5. Parameter Estimation & QBER: Alice and Bob sacrifice a random sample of sifted bits to estimate the Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER). Under an intercept-resend attack, Eve induces an expected QBER of $25\%$.
  6. Key Distillation: If $\text{QBER} < 11\%$ (the Shor-Preskill threshold), error correction and privacy amplification extract an information-theoretically secure final key with positive rate $R \ge 1 - 2h(\text{QBER})$. Otherwise, the protocol is aborted.

Realistic Quantum Noise Modeling

Information-Theoretic Security Metrics
